\subsubsection{Is it really a platform?}

A MSP is a multisided platform that lets two or more distinct groups interact directly, and each group is affiliated with the platform (Øverby
and Audestad \parencite[p.~150]{overby2021}). This test is important for S3NS. The core PREMI3NS looks like a pipeline. S3NS licences Google Cloud Technology, runs it in France under French control and sells capacity to clients. If that were all, S3NS would be a sovereign reseller, not a platform. 

The lecture "Network Economics\_PathDependency\_MultisidedPlatforms\_2026" separates an MSP from a reseller \cite[slide 40]{windekilde2026msp} \parencite[p.~150]{overby2021})MJAU. A reseller buys from an producer and sells to a buyer, and the two never interact. An MSP enables direct itneraction between groups that are all affiliated with it \parencite[p.~150]{overby2021}). For its core service, S3NSlooks like a reseller: it licences Google Cloud Technology and sells capacity to clients, and Google never meets the client. We argue that PREMI3NS is a hybrid. On top of this reseller core sits a platform layer where clients and software vendors deal with each other directly. SAP, for example, sells its ERP to course lists cloud services among technology platforms \cite[slide 36]{windekilde2026msp}.

Two actors are often wrongly treated as sides. Google supplies technology to the alliance and is paid for it, but does not transact with clients through the platform. ANSSI sets the rules for who may enter the trusted perimeter. Both shape the platform from outside the market it creates. 

\subsubsection{The sides}

\textbf{Side 1: Client institutions.} This side is divided into two groups with their own need. The first is French state administration. When they host particularly sensitive
data with a private cloud provider, they must use a service qualified by ANSSI
(in practice SecNumCloud) or certified at EU level. This requirement was written
into law through article 31 of the 2024 SREN law, with an implementing decree
adopted in April 2026 \parencite{decretsren2026}. The second group is large companies in regulated sectors. Around ~30 early adopters tested the PREMI3NS before qualification, and EDF and Thales itself are named users \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. For these firms, trusted cloud is a strategic choice and not a legal duty. Both groups face high switching costs once they have migrated. Their value proporsition is modern cloud services that can legally host sensitive data and are shielded from extraterritorial law. 



\textbf{Side 2: Complementors.} These are ISVs, SaaS providers and service partners (integrator and consultancies). They must invest to port to qualify their products, and most can also offer them on competing trusted clouds, so they multi-home. An industry analysis describes the ISV model on PREMI3NS as still at an early stage \parencite{futurum2026}. This means the platform does not yet have a large complementor side, which is exactly the chicken-and-egg problem MSP theory describes. Their value proporsition is access to regulated clients they could not otherwise serve, without building their own qualified infrastructure.

\subsubsection{Pricing Architecture}

MSP Theory states that the platform should subsidise the side that is more price sensitive and creates more value for the other side, and it should charge the side whose demand is less elastic \parencite{rochet2006, parker2005}. A new platform must also get one side on board first to solve the chicken-and-egg problem \parencite{caillaud2003}. 

The client side is partly documented. S3NS published a catalogue price list in euros. Each service is billed per unit used, for instance per vCPU hour or per gigabyte per month. Many services offer a free usage tier, and clients who commit for one or three years get lower unit prices \parencite{s3nsprices}. For a standard C3 compute core, the one-year price is about 28\% lower and the three-year price about 46\% lower than the pay-as-you-go price. This confirms that the clients are the money side. It also shows a pricing tool that matters for Section~\ref{sec:PathDependency}. The commitment discount trades a lower-price today for a longer tie to the platform, so it strengthens lock-in. Clients with sensitive data accept this because the law and the small number of qualified providers make their demand less price elastic. Private firms have more choice, and negotiated commitments let S3NS offer them a lower effective price than state buyers who have to use a qualified cloud. 

The complementor side is not documented, so we build it from theory. Since the ISV part is still small \parencite{futurum2026} and vendors multi-home, they should be subsidised through low or zero platform fees, technical help with porting and support toward qualification (A2). The SAP deal shows another kind of subsidy. Thales, one of the alliance partners, became the first customer of SAP on PREMI3NS \parencite{thalessap2026}. This guaranteed demand lowers SAP's risk of joining. The alliance uses one of its own partners as an anchor client to attract the other side.

These prices are likely to change over time. Once clients are locked in a single-home for each workload (A4), competitive bottleneck theory predicts that the platform controls access to them and can start charging the multi-homing side \parencite{armstrong2006}. S3NS could then introduce fees for listing and certifying vendor solutions. The vendor subsidy is therefore likely to be temporary. 

The alliance structure decides who funds the subsidy. Client revenue flows to S3NS, and part of it goes to Google as licence fees (A3). Google therefore shares in the growth of the paying side, while the cost of subsidising vendors falls mostly on Thales, through S3NS and through its own role as anchor client. At the same time, software built for PREMI3NS uses Google technology, so Google gains from the subsidy without paying for it. This uneven split is a real tension inside the alliance. It also matters for Section~\ref{sec:task4}, because the clients who pay are the ones who carry most of the loss if the platform is breached. 

\subsubsection{Assumptions}

\begin{description}
  \item[A1.] PREMI3NS unit prices include a premium over standard Google Cloud prices for comparable services. We did not compare the two price lists line by line. The assumption rests on the extra cost of sovereign operation: separate infrastructure, French staff and quarantine zone where every Google update is checked before use \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}.
  \item[A2.] Vendors are price sensitive and multi-home. The ISV model is still young \parencite{futurum2026}, and vendors can also qualify their products on competing trusted clouds.
  \item[A3.] Google is paid through the licence fees that grow with usage. The contract is not public. If the fee were fixed instead, Thales would carry all the volume risk. 
  \item[A4.] After migration, each client workload effectively single-homes, because of the switching costs identified in Section~\ref{sec:PathDependency} and the multi-year commitments in the price list.
\end{description}
