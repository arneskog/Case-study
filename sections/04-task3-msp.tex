\subsection{Task 3: Multisided Platform Mapping}
\label{sec:task3}

\subsubsection{Is it really a platform?}

An MSP lets two or more distinct groups interact directly, and all groups are affiliated with the platform \parencite[p.~150]{overby2021}. A reseller, by contrast, buys from a producer and sells to a buyer, and the two never interact \parencite[slide 40]{windekilde2026msp}. S3NS licenses Google Cloud technology, runs it in France and sells capacity to clients, and Google never meets the client. For its core service, S3NS is therefore a sovereign reseller.

We argue that PREMI3NS is a hybrid. On top of the reseller core sits a platform layer where service partners and independent software vendors (ISVs) deal directly with clients \parencite{s3nsecosystem}. The clearest example is SAP, which agreed to run its RISE private cloud ERP on PREMI3NS \parencite{thalessap2026}. SAP joins to reach regulated clients, and clients join partly because such software is available inside the trusted perimeter. Each group's value depends on the size of the other, which is the defining feature of an MSP \parencite[p.~150]{overby2021}.

Google and ANSSI are often wrongly treated as sides. Google supplies technology and is paid for it, and ANSSI sets the rules for entering the trusted perimeter. Neither transacts with clients on the platform, so both shape it from outside (Figure~\ref{fig:msp}).

\subsubsection{The sides}

\textbf{Side 1: Client institutions.} This side has two groups. The first is the French state administration, which must use a service qualified by ANSSI (in practice SecNumCloud) or certified at EU level when it hosts particularly sensitive data with a private provider. This was written into law through Article 31 of the 2024 SREN law, with an implementing decree adopted in April 2026 \parencite{decretsren2026}. The second is large companies in regulated sectors: around 30 early adopters tested PREMI3NS before qualification, and EDF and Thales itself are named users \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. For them, trusted cloud is a strategic choice, not a legal duty. Both face high switching costs after migrating. Their value proposition is modern cloud services that can legally host sensitive data and are shielded from extraterritorial law.

\textbf{Side 2: Complementors.} These are ISVs, SaaS providers and service partners (integrators and consultancies). They must invest to port and qualify their products and can also offer them on competing trusted clouds, so they multi-home. The ISV model is still at an early stage \parencite{futurum2026}, which is the chicken-and-egg problem of MSP theory \parencite{caillaud2003}. Their value proposition is access to regulated clients without building their own qualified infrastructure.

\subsubsection{Cross-side and same-side effects}

Table~\ref{tab:effects} classifies the effects using the course typology \parencite[slides 14--23]{windekilde2026msp}. The strongest effect runs from clients to vendors. Like app developers on iOS, vendors respond to the size of the installed base \parencite[slide 19]{windekilde2026msp}, and every new regulated client enlarges a market that barely existed before qualification. The reverse effect is weaker, since many clients build their own applications. While the vendor side is thin, the mechanism can also run backwards: too few applications keep clients away, and too few clients keep vendors away, the ``app gap'' that hurt Windows Phone \parencite[slide 23]{windekilde2026msp}.

Same-side effects are weak. Clients do not interact through the platform (Section~\ref{sec:ValueGrowth}), so there is only a weak bandwagon effect from reference clients, and more vendors mean more competition for the same clients \parencite[slide 40]{windekilde2026msp}. Disintermediation is not a real threat \parencite[slide 27]{windekilde2026msp}, since sensitive data cannot leave a qualified cloud. The real risk is that vendors multi-home on rival qualified clouds such as Bleu.

\subsubsection{Pricing Architecture}

MSP theory says to subsidise the side that is more price sensitive and creates more value for the other side, and to charge the side whose demand is less elastic \parencite{rochet2006, parker2005}. A new platform must first get one side on board \parencite{caillaud2003}, and free pricing is the standard way to overcome slow initial adoption \parencite[slide 3]{windekilde2026msp}.

S3NS publishes a price list in euros, billed per unit, for instance per vCPU hour or per gigabyte per month, with free usage tiers for many services \parencite{s3nsprices}. Clients who commit for one or three years get lower prices: for a standard C3 compute core, about 28\% and 46\% below pay-as-you-go. Clients are therefore the money side, and the discount buys a longer tie to the platform, which strengthens lock-in (Section~\ref{sec:PathDependency}). Because the list is the same for everyone, we separate the two client groups with theory. State administrations have legally required, price-inelastic demand and pay list prices. Regulated firms choose trusted cloud strategically and can turn to other qualified clouds, so their demand is more elastic and they can expect negotiated discounts (A5).

The complementor side is not documented, so we build it from theory. Since the ISV side is small \parencite{futurum2026} and vendors multi-home, they should be subsidised through low or zero fees and porting help (A2). The SAP deal is another kind of subsidy: Thales became SAP's first customer on PREMI3NS \parencite{thalessap2026}, guaranteeing demand. In course terms, the alliance pools its installed base to reach critical mass \parencite[slide 38]{windekilde2026msp}. Table~\ref{tab:pricing} summarises the architecture.

These prices are likely to change. Once clients are locked in and single-home (A4), competitive bottleneck theory predicts that the platform can start charging the multi-homing side \parencite{armstrong2006}, so the vendor subsidy is likely to be temporary.

The alliance structure decides who funds the subsidy. Part of client revenue flows to Google as licence fees (A3), so Google shares in the growth of the paying side, while the cost of subsidising vendors falls mostly on Thales, through S3NS and as anchor client. Yet software built for PREMI3NS uses Google technology, so Google gains without paying. This uneven split is a real tension inside the alliance, and it matters for Section~\ref{sec:task4}, because the clients who pay carry most of the loss if the platform is breached.

\subsubsection{Assumptions}

\begin{description}
  \item[A1.] PREMI3NS unit prices include a premium over Google Cloud prices, because of sovereign operation: separate infrastructure, French staff and a quarantine zone for Google updates \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. We did not compare the price lists line by line.
  \item[A2.] Vendors are price sensitive and multi-home. The ISV model is still young \parencite{futurum2026}, and vendors can qualify on competing trusted clouds.
  \item[A3.] Google is paid through usage-based licence fees. The contract is not public; with a fixed fee, Thales would carry all the volume risk.
  \item[A4.] After migration, each client workload effectively single-homes, because of the switching costs identified in Section~\ref{sec:PathDependency} and the multi-year commitments in the price list.
  \item[A5.] State administrations pay list prices, while large regulated firms may obtain negotiated discounts. The price list does not disclose negotiated terms, so this follows the elasticity logic of MSP theory \parencite{rochet2006}.
\end{description}
