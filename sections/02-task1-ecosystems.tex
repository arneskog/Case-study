\subsection{Task 1: Business Ecosystems}
\label{sec:task1}

A business ecosystem is a community of firms that ``coevolve capabilities
around a new innovation'', working both cooperatively and competitively
\parencite[p.~46]{overby2021}. In the digital economy, the ecosystem describes
the relations and dependencies between digital services, ICT infrastructures,
digital markets and authorities \parencite[p.~48]{overby2021}. Following
\textcite{adner2017}, we start from the value proposition rather than from one
firm: cloud services built on Google technology, operated under French control
and qualified for sensitive data. The ecosystem consists of the actors that must
align for this proposition to work.

Figure~\ref{fig:ecosystem} maps the ecosystem around this value proposition.
The alliance itself sits in the blue box in the middle. Direct stakeholders,
shown as grey boxes below it, take part in delivering or using the service.
Thales owns and controls S3NS, which operates PREMI3NS in France, while Google Cloud
supplies the underlying technology, including Compute Engine, Google Kubernetes
Engine and BigQuery \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. Client institutions form the
demand side: around thirty early customers, among them MGEN, Matmut, Club Med
and Birdz, use the service, EDF has announced that it has selected S3NS, and
Thales itself is a user \parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. ANSSI is a direct
stakeholder because its SecNumCloud qualification, granted in December 2025,
decides whether the offer can serve sensitive workloads at all
\parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. Finally, complementors such as consultancies,
integrators and independent software vendors help clients migrate and add
their own solutions \parencite{s3nsecosystem}. SAP is the most visible example,
having agreed to run its RISE cloud ERP on PREMI3NS \parencite{thalessap2026}.

Indirect stakeholders shape the conditions of the ecosystem without
transacting on it. The French state acts as lawmaker: under article 31 of the
SREN law and its 2026 decree, administrations holding particularly sensitive
data must use a cloud service qualified by ANSSI or certified at EU level and
protected against access by non-EU authorities \parencite{decretsren2026}.
US authorities are an indirect but defining stakeholder, since the CLOUD Act
lets them require US providers to disclose data stored abroad
\parencite{doj2019cloudact}. This is the reason a French-controlled offer is
needed at all. Competitors such as Bleu, built by Orange and Capgemini on
Microsoft technology \parencite{lemagit2023usf}, and end users whose data is
hosted on the platform complete the picture; end users are left out of the
figure for readability. These indirect stakeholders are the orange boxes at the
top of Figure~\ref{fig:ecosystem}, and their dashed arrows show that they act
on the alliance through rules and competition rather than through transactions.
As \textcite[p.~58]{overby2021} stress, authorities and consumers are easy to
overlook but can determine a firm's survival.

\subsubsection{Interactions and critical relationships}

The most critical relationship is the arrow from S3NS to Google inside the
alliance box in Figure~\ref{fig:ecosystem}. Every Google
technology update is received in a quarantine zone, where S3NS analyses and
validates it before deployment in an environment administered only by S3NS
\parencite{s3ns2025premi3ns}. This link carries both the value of the offer,
modern cloud services, and its main risk. The second critical link is the
arrow down to ANSSI: without qualification, the regulated demand created by the SREN
decree disappears. The third is the curved arrow at the bottom of the figure, between clients
and complementors.
Clients can only move workloads if the software they need is available inside
the trusted perimeter, which is why the SAP agreement matters
\parencite{thalessap2026}.

\subsubsection{Complementarities}

The partners bring resources the other lacks. Google contributes technology it
has already developed at global scale, while Thales contributes French
ownership, security expertise and control. As cloud provider, Google lets S3NS
offer a full service without owning all the production technology itself
\parencite[p.~54]{overby2021}. The complementarity between S3NS and Google is
\emph{unilateral}: S3NS requires Google's technology to function, whereas
Google's business does not depend on S3NS \parencite[p.~114]{overby2021}. This
mirrors the book's example of Skype and Internet service providers, where Skype
depends on the providers but not the other way round
\parencite[p.~115]{overby2021}.
Between clients and complementors, the complementarity is \emph{supermodular}:
more regulated clients make it worthwhile for vendors to qualify their software,
and more available software makes the platform more attractive to clients
\parencite[slides~27--28]{windekilde2026ecosystem}. This side of the ecosystem
is still small, as the ISV model on PREMI3NS is described as nascent
\parencite{futurum2026}.

\subsubsection{Dependencies}

Read with the notation of \textcite[p.~49]{overby2021}, Figure~\ref{fig:ecosystem}
shows two kinds of dependency. Single arrows go from the alliance to Google and
to ANSSI: S3NS depends on them, but they do not depend on S3NS. Double arrows
link the alliance to clients and to complementors, and clients to complementors:
these actors depend on each other.
The dependency on Google is the most serious because the technology is highly
co-specialised and has low fungibility: S3NS cannot switch to another provider
without rebuilding its service \parencite[slide~30]{windekilde2026ecosystem}.
Clients face a similar dependency in turn, since their systems are built on
Google-based services. The dependency on ANSSI is regulatory rather than
technical, but equally critical, since the qualification is the basis of the
offer's value.

\subsubsection{How the alliance changes roles, incentives and influence}

The alliance changes the position of each main actor compared with acting
alone, which is why Thales and Google are drawn inside one box in
Figure~\ref{fig:ecosystem} rather than as separate firms. Google moves from being a foreign supplier, and a competitor through its
own public cloud, to a technology partner. It gains access to regulated French
demand it could not serve directly, but gives up operational control to S3NS.
Thales moves from security specialist to cloud operator and owner, and also acts
as anchor customer, both on PREMI3NS and as SAP's first customer on the platform
\parencite{thalessap2026}. The French state changes role from buyer to market
maker, since its regulation creates the demand the alliance serves
\parencite{decretsren2026}. The incentives are not fully aligned: Thales must
guarantee independence, while Google benefits from wider use of its technology,
including through the replication of the model in Germany
\parencite{thalesgoogle2026}. Influence in the ecosystem therefore rests on a
paradox. Thales holds formal control, but Google holds the technology that the
whole ecosystem depends on.
