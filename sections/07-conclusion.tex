\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The S3NS alliance shows that sovereignty can be built on a partner's technology, but only partly. Thales gives PREMI3NS French control and the SecNumCloud qualification, while Google supplies the technology that the whole ecosystem depends on (Task 1). This is the central tension: S3NS controls operation and legal exposure, but not the technology underneath.

This split shapes the other findings. Most of the alliance's advantage is supply-side economies of scale, because Google has already paid for the technology. Real network effects exist, but only indirectly between clients and vendors. Lock-in runs on two levels, from client to S3NS and from S3NS to Google, but it does not make the market tip (Task 2). PREMI3NS is a hybrid: a sovereign reseller at the core, with a platform layer where clients pay and vendors are subsidised. Thales funds this subsidy, and since vendors multi-home, it is probably temporary (Task 3).

Security follows the same pattern. The core is close to best shot, but the identity layer, interfaces and vendor applications are weakest links, and breach losses fall mostly on clients and end users, who make no security investment (Task 4). Value is also split unevenly. S3NS earns a sovereignty premium but still makes losses, which Thales carries, while Google owns the technology and earns as volume grows (Task 5).

Overall, the alliance gives clients a legally qualified, French-operated cloud, but not technical independence.
